PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION

Water hardness is a common issue in building management, affecting both the efficiency of water systems and the longevity of appliances. Hard water contains high levels of calcium and magnesium ions, which can lead to scale buildup in pipes and fixtures, reducing water flow and increasing maintenance costs. Understanding and managing water hardness is crucial for ensuring optimal performance and sustainability in building operations.

DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS

System Component: Flow Rate Sensor Integration Primary Function The flow rate sensor continuously monitors the volumetric flow of incoming hard, unsoftened water (ground or surface water) entering the treatment system. While standard environmental sensors provide baseline metrics, the flow rate sensor serves as the primary operational safeguard for system capacity and data validation. Key Functional Roles Resin Protection & Treatment Capacity Control: Softening units operate within specific flow rate thresholds (typically 0–45 GPM). Excessively high flow rates reduce contact time with the ion-exchange resin, leading to incomplete hardness removal and premature resin exhaustion. Real-time monitoring ensures incoming volume remains within optimal treatment limits. Cross-Sensor Malfunction & Anomaly Detection: By correlating real-time volumetric data with post-treatment hardness readings (e.g., via conductivity or ion-selective sensors), the system performs automated health checks: Zero-Flow Anomaly: A zero flow rate reading paired with an active hardness reading (>15 ppm sustained for >5 minutes) flags an explicit sensor malfunction. Discrepancy Checks: Mismatched flow vs. hardness profiles highlight mechanical failures, such as stuck valves or erratic tap control of incoming hard water. Over-Saturation Risk Prevention: If flow rates remain within normal parameters (0–45 GPM) but hardness surges (>80 ppm), the system flags an insufficient sodium ion exchanger state—preventing unsoftened water from bypassing effective treatment. Data Correlation & Regional Scalability: Continuous flow volume tracking provides the empirical data required to model mathematical correlations between dynamic flow rates and hardness concentrations. This predictive telemetry is critical for scaling the IoT sensor package across multi-building infrastructure across regions (e.g., Kitchener-Waterloo area water networks).

FINAL SOLUTION

The final solution involves the implementation of three real-time hardware sensors (brine level, conductivity, flow rate), connected through an IoT infrastrucutre to the SmartSoft prototype app. Building management or facility teams will have access to water quality measurements in-real-time.

APP PROTOTYPE: SMARTSOFT

The SmartSoft app is designed to provide building management teams with real-time water quality data, including hardness levels, brine tank status, and flow rates. The app features a user-friendly interface that allows for easy monitoring and management of the water softening system. Alerts and notifications are sent to the management team in case of any anomalies or maintenance requirements.

HARDWARE SENSORS

The hardware sensors used in the SmartSoft system include a brine level sensor, a conductivity sensor, and a flow rate sensor. These sensors are strategically placed within the water softening system to provide accurate and reliable data on water quality. The brine level sensor monitors the salt levels in the brine tank, the conductivity sensor measures the electrical conductivity of the water to determine hardness levels, and the flow rate sensor tracks the volume of water passing through the system.

Flow rate sensor

The flow rate sensor measures the rate of water entering the water softener, providing information about how much water is passing through the system over time. Two sensing approaches were considered: 1. Turbine / Impeller Sensor with Magnetic Pickup An inline turbine rotates as water flows through the pipe. A magnet embedded in the turbine is detected by a Hall-effect sensor, producing electrical pulses as the turbine rotates. By counting these pulses, the system can determine the water flow rate, typically reported in L/min or m³/h. 2. Ultrasonic Time-of-Flight Sensor An ultrasonic sensor measures flow by detecting changes in the travel time of ultrasonic signals through the moving water. Unlike the turbine-based approach, the sensor can be mounted externally on the pipe using a clamp. Placement considerations: The turbine sensor is installed inline with the water supply, while the ultrasonic sensor can be externally clamped onto the pipe. This distinction was an important consideration when evaluating potential sensor configurations for the system.
